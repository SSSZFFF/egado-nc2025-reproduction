Supplementary Note 1: An overview of the EG-ADO 1 
As inverse design continues to evolve within the field of photonics, numerous 2 
methodologies have been developed and widely adopted. These inverse -designed 3 
devices based on metamater ials can generally be categorized into analog and digital 4 
metamaterials. Analog metamaterials can be optimized using gradient -based topology 5 
optimization algorithms, with the adjoint method being the most prevalent 1. This 6 
method calculates the optimization sensitivity of design parameters in the focused  7 
region through two electromagnetic simulations, allowing efficient inverse design by 8 
updating these parameters with a suitable optimizer. The two simulations include a 9 
forward simulation using the incident source as excitation and an adjoint sim ulation 10 
using reciprocal displacement current as excitation source 2. However, gradient-based 11 
optimization often requires fine electromagnetic computation grids and results in 12 
devices with irregular and extremely small features, posing significant challenges for 13 
reliable fabrication tolerance. In contrast, digital metamaterials 3,4, with their larger 14 
minimum feature sizes (>100 nm) and more regular geometries , offer enhanced  15 
fabrication stability, makin g them well -suited for large -scale production through 16 
commercial foundries.  Nonetheless, traditional optimization methods for digital 17 
metamaterials are inefficient and time-intensive5.   18 
The edge-guided analog-and-digital optimization (EG-ADO) method proposed in 19 
this paper combines the efficiency of topology optimization with the fabrication 20 
robustness of digital designs. It extracts edge information from analog patterns using 21 
image edge detection techniques and uses this information to guide the transformation 22 
from analog to digital patterns, achieving efficient and low -loss conversion. The EG -23 
ADO method consists of three main stages , detailed step-by-step in this section. For a 24 
detailed illustration of the pattern conversion process during optimization, please refer 25 
to Supplementary Note 4. 26

Following the optimization of analog metamaterials  using the adjoint method, the 11 
permittivity distribution within the design region, termed the analog pattern, is extracted 12 
and processed using the Canny edge detection algorithm7. Given that the analog pattern 13 
can be regarded as a single- channel grayscale image, the workflow  for Canny edge 14 
detection in this wo rk includes smoothing with a Gaussian filter, directional gradient 15 
calculation using the Sobel operator, non-maximum suppression, double thresholding, 16 
and finally edge tracking by hysteresis. Fig. S1(b) displays the normalized permittivity 17 
distribution of a two-mode MUX after topology optimization, and Fig. S1(c) shows the 18 
edge map obtained using the Canny edge detector, where white pixels (value of 1) 19 
indicate edges and black pixels (value of 0) indicate non-edge areas. 20 
Due to the small pixel size of the analog pattern (20 nm×20 nm in this design) 21 
relative to the significantly larger pixel size required for the target digital pattern (120  22 
nm×120 nm in this design), a pooling operation is required to adjust the pixel 23 
dimensions, as depicted in Fig. S1(d). Max pooling is employed, where the pooling 24 
kernel is an all -one matrix. The scaling factor of pooling can be expressed as S AD = 25 
PA/PD, where P A and PD are the pixel side length of the analog and digital patterns, 26 
respectively. This process convolves the  final edge map with the pooling kernel to 27

generate a feature map  (Fig. S1( e)). Subsequently, this feature map undergoes 1

The index mapping is then conducted based on the decision matrix. Direct mapping of 5 
the index is only performed if the corresponding region in the analog pattern consists 6

determined”). 8 
Additionally, to discuss the relationship between conversion efficiency and 9 
optimization performance, we introduce a dilation coefficient D  which determines the 10 
size of the pooling kernel as S AD+D. Increasing D raises the proportion of TBD pixels 11 
and can be visually understood as thickening the boundaries (see Supplementary Note 12 
4). When D is greater than 0, as illustrated in Fig. S1(d), the edge map is surrounded by 13 
padding of width D/2. The stride of the pooling operation, set to the scaling factor SAD, 14 
facilitates the conversion of the pixel size of the analog pattern to that required for the 15 
digital pattern. 16 
C. Digital optimization based on the direct binary search method 17 
Following the index mapping based on the decision map, we obtain the converted 18 
pattern of the device, which includes pixels directly mapped to Si and SiO 2, as well as 19 
pixels where the material remains to be determined. Subsequently, we employ the direct 20 
binary search (DBS) method to specifically determine the materials for TBD pixels. For 21 
each TBD pixel, its index is alternately set to 𝑛𝑛𝑆𝑆𝑆𝑆 = 3.48 and 𝑛𝑛𝑆𝑆𝑆𝑆𝑂𝑂2 = 1.44 , and one 22 
3D FDTD simulation is conducted for each setting to calculate the FOM. The material 23 
that yields the better  FOM is then selected for that pixel. After a complete round of 24 
iteration through all TBD pixels, the optimization concludes. The pixel traversal during 25 
this optimization is conducted in a column-wise direction. The detailed workflow of the 26 
DBS method can be found in Reference8. 27 
D. Performance evolution during the optimization process 28 
To better illustrate the optimization process, we present a graph showing the evolution 29 
of device performance and fabrication difficulty during both the topology optimization 30 
(TO) and digital optimization (DO) stages (see Fig. S 2), for the five -mode MUX. 31 
Device performance is quantified by the insertion loss across five mode channels. Note 32 
that the insertion loss here refers to the mean value calculated across 11 evenly spaced 33 
wavelength points (ranging from 1530 to 1570 nm). Fabrication difficulty is represented 34 
by the proportion of edge pixels, with a higher proportion indicating greater sensitivity 35 
to fabrication variations. During the initial phase of the TO stage (i.e., grayscale 36 
optimization), the d evice performance improves rapidly due to the efficiency of the 37 
adjoint method, with insertion loss dropping to near zero. However, since no index 38 
constraints are applied at this phase, the proportion of edge pixels increases sharply, 39 
exceeding 60%. In the subsequent binarization phase of TO, the edge pixel proportion 40 
is reduced to about 25%, and the device performance slightly degrades. Although this 41 
binarization phase helps mitigate structure complexity, it is insufficient for ensuring 42

E. Estimation of optimization time consumption 12 
To estimate the optimization time consumption of the traditional DBS method and our 13 
proposed EG-ADO method, we first measure the time required for a single run of 3D -14 
FDTD simulations for the 5- mode and 6- mode MUXs individually on a desktop 15 
computer. The specifications of the hardware and the  simulation settings are provided 16 
in the Supplementary Table 1. The results show that for the 5- mode MUX, the 17 
simulation time using CPU and GPU is 274 seconds and 68 seconds, respectively. For 18 
the more complex 6 -mode MUX with a larger simulation domain, the time is 880 19 
seconds and 90 seconds for CPU and GPU, respectively.  20 
Based on these measurements, we use the fitted results from Fig. 2(e)  in the main 21 
text to calculate the required number of days for designing the 5- mode and 6-mode 22 
MUXs using the DBS algorithm on both CPU and GPU. Additionally, we calculate the 23 
optimization time consumption when using the GPU with the novel EG-ADO algorithm. 24 
The results are presented in Fig. S3. When utilizing the CPU with the DBS algorithm, 25 
the optimization time consumptions for the two MUX designs are approximately 298 26 
days and 1,767 days, respectively, which are highly impractical. With GPU acceleration, 27 
the required time consumptions are reduced to 74 days and 180 days, respectively, 28

Supplementary Note 2: Calculation of mode effective index 1 
To determine the appropriate widths for the input and output waveguides of the mode 2 
MUXs, we use the FDE solver of ANSYS Lumerical MODE ( Fig. S4(a)) to calculate 3 
the effective index of various orthogonal modes. We conduct two- dimensional 4 
simulations, assuming the waveguide is infinitely long in the direction of light 5 
propagation, and set the boundary conditions on all four sides to perfectly matched 6 
layers (PMLs). The background index is set to 1.44. For the waveguide core, we set the 7 
height to 220 nm and the material to silicon, and sweep the width from 300 nm to 3000 8 
nm to determine the effective indices of different modes at various widths. Around the 9 
waveguide, we use a small mesh size of 5×11 nm². The two-dimensional set-up exhibits 10 
a fast computational speed, facilitating the implementation of a very fine mesh to obtain 11 
accurate indices. Fig. S4(b) shows the evolution in the effective indices of the first six 12 
TE modes at the wavelength of 1550 nm. As the width increases, high- order modes 13 
gradually appear. We set the width of the single -mode waveguide at 500 nm, and the 14 
widths for the  multimode waveguides in two-mode, four-mode, five-mode, and six-15 
mode MUX s are sequentially set to 900 nm, 1800 nm, 2500 nm, and 3000 nm, 16 
respectively, to accommodate the corresponding modes. Fig. S4(c) depicts the mode 17 
profiles for TE0 to TE5 modes at a waveguide width of 3000 nm  as the wavelength is 18 
set to 1550 nm. 19

Supplementary Note 8: Strategies for system scaling and flexibility 1 
A. Potential approach for higher integration in system 2 
Leveraging existing solutions for  advanced photonic devices, we can achieve greater 3 
integration level for CMOS -compatible multi- dimensional on- chip optical 4 
interconnects. Recent demonstrations 24,25 show that signal transmission based on 5 
integrated optical frequency combs incurs only minimal power penalties compared to 6 
tunable lasers, making them a viable alternative to bulky laser systems. Reports on 7 
integrated turn-key microcombs26 and electrically-driven mode-locked microcombs27 8 
offer promising prospects in terms of operational stability and reconfigurability.  9 
In addition to optical frequency combs, compact microring modulators (MRMs) 10 
present another avenue for enhancing integration. The inherent wavelength- selective 11 
properties of MRMs, derived from cavity r esonance, allow them to be cascaded to 12 
generate wavelength -division multiplexing (WDM) signals. Both 300 Gb s -1 13 
interconnects based on single MRM 28 and potential terabit -level signal modulation 14 
using cascaded MRMs 29 have been successfully demonstrated. An energy- efficient 15 
MRM30 capable of operating under 1V has also been proposed, allowing direct drive 16 
by CMOS electronics.  17 
The integration challenge is not limited to on -chip devic es but also extends to 18 
efficient signal coupling and transmission across different system levels. Innovations 19 
in low-loss, wide-bandwidth multimode chip-to-fiber couplers31 and multimode fibers32 20 
have enabled flexible transmission of multi -dimensional signals across server racks in 21 
datacenters. These advancements address critical interconnect needs at various 22 
hierarchical levels, supporting efficient, high-capacity data transmission in large-scale 23 
computing systems. 24 
B. Flexibly routed MDM circuits 25 
The proposed digital metamaterial- based mode MUXs can be seamlessly combined 26 
with on- chip multimode signal processing/switching 33,34 and routing elements 35, to 27 
further enhance functionality. However, flexible routing for multimode waveguides 28 
necessitates the use of multimode bends. Conventional  multimode waveguide bends 29
